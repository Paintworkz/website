How to Price 2 Pac Cabinet Doors

Per square metre, per door or per linear metre — which one to use, what to add on top, and how to work out what a square metre really costs your shop.

By Colin Bedford, Paintworkz — a 2 pac spray shop in Hamilton, Victoria · Updated November 2026

Every spray shop prices doors a little differently, and most of us learned it the hard way: a job that looked fine on the quote and lost money on the floor. Usually it isn't the rate that's wrong — it's the way the job was measured, or something that never made it onto the quote: a shaker profile, a double-sided panel, a colour match.

This guide covers the three common ways to price 2 pac work, when each one makes sense, and the extras that are easy to forget.

The three ways to charge

Per square metre: the fairest default

Pricing by area is the most common method in 2 pac shops because it scales with what actually costs you money: paint, sanding time and gun time all go up with the size of the part.

How to measure

  • Measure each part's height × width in millimetres and convert: 720 × 450 mm = 0.324 m².
  • Add up every part in the job, grouped by profile and finish, then multiply by your rate for that profile and finish.
  • Double-sided parts count twice. If both faces get painted (open shelving, island back panels, glass-door frames seen from both sides), charge for both sides. Most kitchen doors are single-sided, with pull film or a melamine back.

Why profile matters

A plain flat door and a shaker door of the same size are very different jobs. Shakers, V-grooves and routed profiles need hand sanding into corners and tracks, and that prep can take several times longer than a flat panel. Set a separate rate per m² for each profile rather than one rate for everything — a small premium on plain doesn't cover a profile that takes three times the prep.

Why finish matters

Matt, satin, semi-gloss, gloss and clear coats don't cost the same to produce. Gloss shows every flaw and usually means more cutting back and more coats. Price each finish separately too.

Per door: simple, but check the sizes

A flat price per door is easy for customers to understand and quick to quote — handy for homeowners comparing kitchen resprays. It works well when the doors are all roughly the same size.

The catch is the outliers. A 2100 mm pantry door has three or four times the area of a standard base door. If you charge per door, either have size bands (standard, tall, drawer front) or charge oversize parts by the m².

Per linear metre: for long, narrow parts

Kickboards, fillers, scribe strips and mouldings are awkward to price by area — they're narrow, so the m² looks tiny, but they still have to be handled, sanded and sprayed along their whole length. A rate per linear metre (measured on the long edge) fixes that.

Keep it for genuinely narrow parts. Use it on a wide panel and you'll give away paint and labour.

The extras that get forgotten

  • Colour matching — matching a sample or an existing kitchen takes time and test sprays. Charge a set fee per colour.
  • Small jobs — a few doors still need the gun cleaned, paint mixed and a booth cycle. A minimum charge or small-job fee stops tiny orders losing money.
  • Glass-door frames — there's less area than a solid door but extra masking and fiddly edges. Many shops charge a percentage of the solid-door rate rather than measuring the frame.
  • Edges — painted or pencil-rounded edges on cut-to-size work add handling time; price them separately if you do them.
  • Delivery and freight — set rates by region so they're on every quote, not added from memory.
  • Repairs and prep — damaged or previously painted doors (resprays) need more sanding and filling than new MDF.

Work out what a square metre really costs you

A rate only works if it's above your cost. Three quick numbers tell you where you stand:

  1. Materials per m² = your monthly paint, primer, hardener, thinners and sandpaper spend ÷ the m² you painted that month. Many shops are surprised by this one — when volume drops, the cost per m² climbs.
  2. Labour per m² = wages for the month ÷ m² painted.
  3. Overheads per m² = rent, power, booth filters, insurance and the rest ÷ m² painted.

Add the three together. That's your break-even per m². Your plain-profile rate needs to sit comfortably above it, and your profile and gloss rates above that. If your break-even is close to your rate, you're working for nothing on every quiet month.

Re-check it whenever paint prices go up, or when you're busier or quieter than usual.

Quick checklist before you send a quote

  • Every part measured, grouped by profile and finish
  • Double-sided parts counted twice
  • Narrow parts on a linear-metre rate
  • Oversize doors not priced as standard
  • Colour match, small-job fee, glass frames and delivery added
  • Your rates still above your break-even per m²
