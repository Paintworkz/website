Tracking Parts Through a Spray Shop

Count in, count out, label every part — and stop the "we’re one door short" phone calls.

By Colin Bedford, Paintworkz — a 2 pac spray shop in Hamilton, Victoria · Updated November 2026

Every spray shop has had the phone call: "We're one door short." Somewhere between the truck, the prep bench, the racks and the packing table, a part went missing — or got painted the wrong colour, or went home with the wrong job.

Losing parts is rarely about careless staff. It's about parts that all look the same, moving through a busy shop with nothing on them to say whose they are.

Count in, count out

The single most useful habit: count every job against the order when it arrives, before anything is sanded. If the order says 36 parts and 35 came in, you find out now — not after you've painted 35 and the client blames you for the 36th.

  • Check sizes against the order as well as the count. A 600 door that should be 450 is the joiner's problem today, and yours next week.
  • Note damage on arrival — chips, dents, swollen edges — and photograph it.
  • Count again at packing, against the same list.

Label every part

Once a door is primed, it looks like every other primed door. A label on the back (or on a hidden edge) that stays with the part through the shop solves most mix-ups. A good part label shows:

  • Job number and client
  • Part number, out of the total (for example, 14 of 36)
  • Size and description (for example, "Pantry door 2100 × 600")
  • Colour, finish and profile
  • Whether it's single or double-sided

Print them on adhesive label sheets so they're quick to apply and easy to read.

Use a job sticker on the rack or trolley

Part labels tell you what a door is. A larger job sticker on the rack, trolley or stack tells everyone what the whole group is — job, client, colour, due date and part count. Staff can see at a glance what's on each rack without turning doors over.

A supply docket that matches the job

When the job leaves, it should go with a docket that lists every part, its size and colour, and the total count. The client signs for what they received. If there's ever an argument about a missing door, the docket settles it.

Keep the job record together

The order, the measurements, the colour formula, the labels, the docket and the invoice should all come from the same record. When they're typed into different places, that's where the mistakes creep in — a size typed wrong on the label but right on the invoice, or a colour changed on one and not the other.

The rule of thumb: measure once, and print everything else from that. Labels, dockets, mixing cards and invoices should never be retyped.
