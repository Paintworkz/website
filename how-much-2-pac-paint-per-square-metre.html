How Much 2 Pac Paint per Square Metre?

Work it out from your data sheet, allow for what never reaches the part, and mix to the job instead of guessing.

By Colin Bedford, Paintworkz — a 2 pac spray shop in Hamilton, Victoria · Updated November 2026

"How much paint do I need?" sounds like a simple question, but in a 2 pac shop it decides whether you run out halfway through a job, or end up with a tin of mixed colour going off on the bench. Mixed 2 pac has a pot life, so you can't keep it for next week the way you can with a single-pack paint.

Here's how to work it out properly, using numbers from your paint supplier's data sheet instead of guesswork.

Step 1: theoretical coverage from the data sheet

Every 2 pac primer and topcoat has a technical data sheet (TDS). Two numbers on it give you the theoretical coverage:

  • Volume solids (%) — how much of the mixed paint stays on the part after the solvent flashes off.
  • Dry film thickness (DFT) per coat, in microns (µm) — how thick each coat should be once dry.

Theoretical coverage (m² per litre) = volume solids % × 10 ÷ DFT in µm

Example: a topcoat with 50% volume solids sprayed at 40 µm dry: 50 × 10 ÷ 40 = 12.5 m² per litre, per coat.

That's the number for a perfect world, where every drop lands on the part. Spraying doesn't work like that.

Step 2: allow for what doesn't land on the part

Overspray, edges, gun cleaning and paint left in the cup all come off the top. How much depends mostly on the gun:

Spray methodTypical transfer efficiencyWhat it means
Conventional air sprayRoughly 25–40%Most of the paint misses the part
HVLPAround 65%Common in cabinet shops
Air-assisted airlessAround 50–70%Faster on large runs

Flat doors on a rack waste more than a big panel, because a lot of the spray pattern goes past the edges. Small parts and drawer fronts are the worst.

Real coverage = theoretical coverage × transfer efficiency

12.5 m²/L × 65% = about 8 m² per litre per coat. That's a much more honest figure to plan with.

Once you've run a few jobs, compare what you mixed with the m² you sprayed. Your own shop's figure beats any formula.

Step 3: count the coats and the faces

  • Coats: a typical system is a sealer or primer coat (sometimes two on raw MDF edges) and two topcoats. Work out primer and topcoat separately — they have different solids and DFT.
  • Faces: single-sided doors get one face plus edges. Double-sided parts get both faces. Count the area that actually gets paint.
  • Edges: MDF edges soak up more primer than the face. On small parts, edges are a big share of the total.

A worked example

A job with 8.5 m² of single-sided doors, one primer coat and two topcoats. The paint figures below are examples only — use your own data sheets.

CoatAreaReal coverageLitres needed
Primer (1 coat)8.5 m²6 m²/L1.4 L
Topcoat (2 coats)17.0 m²8 m²/L2.1 L
Total3.5 L mixed

Remember that's mixed paint: base plus hardener plus thinners. If the ratio is, say, 2 parts base to 1 part hardener with 10–20% thinners, only part of those litres is the base you buy.

Mixing to the job, not the tin

  • Mix in batches you can spray inside the pot life. A big job is better as two or three mixes than one tin that starts to thicken before the last rack.
  • Tinted colours need extra care. Deep colours carry a lot of tinter. Scale the formula exactly by weight so the second batch matches the first.
  • Write it down. Record the formula, the batch size and the job number. When the client wants two more doors next year, you can match it.
  • Track what you used. Litres mixed against m² sprayed is the number that tells you your real coverage — and your real paint cost per m².

Turning litres into cost per m²

Once you know your real coverage, your paint cost per m² is simple: cost per litre of mixed paint ÷ real coverage, for each coat. Add primer and topcoat, plus sandpaper and thinners for cleaning. That number belongs in your pricing — see How to Price 2 Pac Cabinet Doors.
