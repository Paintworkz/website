How to Price Cabinet Painting

Per door, per square foot or per linear foot — which to use, and what’s easy to leave off the quote.

By Colin Bedford, Paintworkz — a 2 pac spray shop in Hamilton, Victoria · Updated November 2026

Cabinet painting and refinishing is one of the most profitable services a painting business can offer — and one of the easiest to under-price. Two kitchens with the same number of doors can be very different jobs, and a quote that looked great can lose money once you're in the shop.

Here are the three common ways to price cabinet painting, when each makes sense, and what's easy to leave off the quote.

Three ways to charge

MethodHow it worksBest forWatch out for
Per doorA flat price per door or drawer frontHomeowner quotes; kitchens with similar-sized doorsTall pantry doors priced like small ones
Per square footHeight × width of each part, added upShop work, mixed sizes, commercial jobsTiny parts that take as long as big ones
Per linear footLength of the part times a rate per footToe kicks, crown, fillers, trimUsing it on wide parts

Per door: what customers expect

Most homeowners compare cabinet painting quotes per door, so it's worth quoting that way. Just don't treat every door the same. Use size bands:

  • Drawer fronts
  • Standard doors (base and wall)
  • Tall doors (pantry, broom, oven tower)
  • End panels and large panels — price by the square foot

And charge more for profiled doors. A Shaker or raised-panel door takes far longer to sand and spray than a flat slab door.

Per square foot: the fairest for the shop

Measure each part in inches and convert: a 30" × 15" door is 450 sq in, or 3.1 sq ft. Add up the job, group it by profile and sheen, and multiply by your rate for each. If both faces of a part get finished (open shelving, island backs), count both sides.

Per linear foot: for trim and toe kicks

Toe kicks, crown molding, light rail and fillers are narrow, so their square footage is tiny — but they still need handling, sanding and spraying along their whole length. Price them per linear foot, measured on the long edge.

A worked example

One kitchen priced three ways. The rates are made-up examples — use your own.

The job: 16 standard doors (30" × 15"), 2 pantry doors (84" × 24"), 8 drawer fronts (7" × 24") and 16 ft of toe kick. Shaker, satin, one color.

Method (example rates)Price
Flat per door: 26 × $95, plus toe kick 16 ft × $9$2,614
Size bands: 16 × $95, 2 pantry × $260, 8 drawer fronts × $55, plus toe kick$2,624
Per sq ft: 87.3 sq ft × $28, plus toe kick 16 ft × $9$2,589

Here the totals land close together, but look at the parts: the flat price charges $95 for each pantry door, which has more than four times the area of a standard door, and $95 for each drawer front, which has about a third. On a kitchen with more tall doors, the flat price quietly costs you money.

What to add on top

  • Prep and repairs — degreasing, deglossing, filling dents, especially on older kitchens.
  • Boxes and frames — painting cabinet boxes and face frames on site is a separate line from the doors.
  • Color matching and two-tone kitchens (a different island color means two mixes and more masking).
  • Remove and reinstall — taking doors off, labeling them, hinges, and adjusting on reinstall.
  • New hardware holes — filling old holes and drilling new ones.
  • Sales tax — check your state's rules for labor and materials.

Know your cost per square foot

Add up a month of paint, primer, hardener, thinners and sandpaper, then labor and overhead, and divide each by the square feet you finished. That's your break-even per sq ft. Your rates — and your per-door prices — need to sit comfortably above it.
