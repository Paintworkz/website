How to Schedule a Spray Shop

Job stages, cure time, standard lead times and weekly capacity — so the dates you promise are dates you keep.

By Colin Bedford, Paintworkz — a 2 pac spray shop in Hamilton, Victoria · Updated November 2026

Most spray shops don't run out of booth space; they run out of days. A job promised for Friday turns into Tuesday because a sealer coat needed an extra sand, three urgent kitchens landed the same week, and a public holiday was nowhere in the plan.

Here's how to schedule 2 pac work so the dates you give clients are dates you can keep.

Know your stages

Every job goes through the same stages, whatever the size. Writing them down is the first step to scheduling them:

StageWhat happensWhat slows it down
Receive & checkCount parts against the order, check for damageMissing or damaged parts
PrepSand, fill, clean, maskProfiles, resprays, repairs
Prime / sealSealer or primer coat, flash-off and cureCure time, temperature
Sand backCut back primer for a smooth baseProfiles, edge build-up
TopcoatsUsually two coats, with flash-off betweenColour changes, gloss work
Cure & QCFull cure, inspect, touch-upsCold weather, rejects
Pack & dispatchWrap, label, deliver or pick upDelivery runs

Cure time is the hidden constraint

You can't rush 2 pac. Check the data sheet for time to sand, time to recoat and time to handle or pack, and remember those times stretch when it's cold. A job that flows in summer can lose a day or two in a Hamilton winter if the booth isn't heated.

  • Plan the cure into the schedule as working time, not as a gap that "should be fine".
  • Batch jobs in the same colour and finish together where you can — every colour change costs a gun clean and a booth cycle.
  • Keep gloss and dark colours away from dusty prep days.

Set a standard lead time — and stick to it

Pick a default number of working days from receiving the parts to ready, and quote it every time. For example, 15 or 20 working days. Then decide what makes a job longer:

  • Big jobs — for example, anything over 30 m² gets extra days.
  • Particular clients — some regular clients send bigger or more complex work and need a longer default.
  • Complex finishes — high gloss, two-tone, or lots of profiled doors.

A consistent lead time is easier to explain, easier to keep, and stops every job becoming a negotiation.

Count working days, not calendar days

"Three weeks" means nothing if the shop is closed for a week over Christmas. Schedule in working days, and mark public holidays and shop closures on the calendar before you promise dates. It's the most common reason good shops miss deadlines.

Know your weekly capacity

Look back over a few months and work out how many m² you actually finish in a normal week. That's your capacity. When the jobs booked for a week add up to more than that, something will be late — better to know on Monday than Thursday.

A simple rule: keep about 10–20% of each week unbooked. Rejects, re-sprays, urgent jobs from good clients and sick days will fill it.

Make the plan visible

A schedule only works if everyone can see it. A whiteboard is better than nothing; a chart showing each job as a bar across the days — with its stage, its due date and anything running late — is better again. When a client rings asking "where's my job?", you should be able to answer in ten seconds.
